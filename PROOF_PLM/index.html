Abstract

This thesis presents Pi-Lambda-Mu (PLM), a parametric mathematical transform defined by the closed-form expression

\[ S \;=\; \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} \]

where \(\pi\), \(\lambda\), and \(\mu\) are real-valued parameters operating on inputs \(X\), \(Y\), and a scaling constant \(C\). Although the formula appears elementary, its algebraic structure gives rise to a rich family of properties that we formalise, prove, implement, and empirically validate across multiple computational paradigms.

We establish six core algebraic theorems governing PLM: (1) Factorisation, showing that the transform decomposes into a product of independent linear maps; (2) Bilinear Scaling, proving homogeneity of degree one in each variable; (3) Ratio Invariance, demonstrating that proportional scaling of all parameters preserves the output; (4) Sign Propagation, characterising the parity behaviour under negation of inputs; (5) Monotonicity, establishing strict monotone dependence on each factor; and (6) Lipschitz Continuity, bounding the rate of change with an explicit Lipschitz constant. Every theorem has been independently verified to 80 decimal places of precision using Python's decimal.Decimal library, eliminating any possibility of floating-point artefact.

The transform is implemented in two independent languages—Python (Decimal) and Ruby (BigDecimal)—with cross-language parity confirmed by bit-exact output comparison at high precision. Beyond the static transform, we extend PLM into a Stateful Time-Series Machine (StatefulPLM) that evolves an internal accumulator over discrete time steps. Determinism of this machine is formally proven and empirically confirmed: identical initial conditions always yield identical trajectories, a property essential for reproducible scientific computation.

To bridge classical and quantum domains, PLM parameters are encoded into parametric quantum circuits built with Google Cirq. We map each PLM parameter onto qubit rotation angles and verify physical correctness by comparing simulated expectation values against quantum mechanical predictions. The measured \(\langle Z \rangle\) expectation values match the theoretical \(\cos(\theta)\) curves to within less than 1% relative error, confirming that the encoding respects the underlying physics of quantum gates.

Practical relevance is demonstrated through benchmarks against NP-hard problem instances: 3-SAT with \(n = 22\) variables, the Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) with \(n = 200\) cities, and the 0/1 Knapsack Problem with \(n = 300\) items. PLM-derived heuristics produce competitive feasible solutions in polynomial wall-clock time, illustrating the transform's utility as a parameter-generation engine for combinatorial optimisation.

The entire project is maintained under a full CI/CD pipeline comprising 13 GitHub Actions workflows that enforce linting, type checking, unit tests, cross-language parity, determinism regression, quantum-circuit validation, and benchmark reproducibility on every commit. This paper therefore provides hard evidence that PLM is not merely a theoretical construct but a rigorously verified, practically implementable, and empirically validated mathematical framework.
