The treatment is intended to be self-contained and suitable for academic peer review.

2.1 Preliminaries and Notation

Before stating the main results we fix notation and specify the domains of all quantities that appear in the PLM transform. Every symbol defined below retains the same meaning throughout the remainder of this thesis unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Definition 2.1 (PLM Domain and Parameters)

Let the following quantities be given:

  • \(\pi \in \mathbb{R}^{+}\) — the pi-parameter. In the standard instantiation this is Archimedes' constant, \(\pi = 3.14159265358979323846\ldots\)
  • \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{+}\) — the lambda-parameter. In the standard instantiation this is the golden ratio, \(\varphi = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} = 1.61803398874989484820\ldots\)
  • \(\mu \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}\) — the mu-parameter. In the standard instantiation this is Euler's number, \(e = 2.71828182845904523536\ldots\)
  • \(X \in \mathbb{Z}\) — the scaling factor (also called the nonce in cryptographic contexts).
  • \(Y \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}\) — a positive integer derived from a hexadecimal hash digest.
  • \(C \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}\) where \(C = \text{block\_size} + \text{crc\_decimal} > 0\) — a composite scaling constant formed from the block size and the decimal representation of a CRC checksum.

The positivity constraints on \(\pi\), \(\lambda\), \(Y\), and \(C\), together with the non-vanishing constraint on \(\mu\), ensure that the transform is always well-defined (the denominator is never zero) and that the sign of the output is determined solely by the sign of \(X\) under the standard instantiation.

Definition 2.2 (PLM Base Ratio)

The PLM Base Ratio is the dimensionless constant formed from the three fundamental parameters alone:

\[ R(\pi, \lambda, \mu) \;:=\; \frac{\pi \cdot \lambda}{\mu} \]

Under the standard instantiation \((\pi, \lambda, \mu) = (\pi, \varphi, e)\), the numerical value of R is computed in Section 2.8 to 80 decimal places. Because \(\pi\), \(\lambda\), and \(\mu\) are all strictly positive (and \(\mu \neq 0\)), we have \(R > 0\) in the standard case.

Definition 2.3 (PLM Transform)

The PLM Transform is the map \(S : \mathbb{R}^{+} \times \mathbb{R}^{+} \times (\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}) \times \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}^{+} \times \mathbb{Z}^{+} \to \mathbb{R}\) defined by

\[ S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, X, Y, C) \;:=\; \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} \]

Expanding the numerator yields the equivalent expression

\[ S \;=\; \frac{\pi \cdot \lambda \cdot X \cdot Y}{\mu \cdot C} \;=\; R(\pi, \lambda, \mu) \;\cdot\; \frac{X \cdot Y}{C} \]

The second form separates S into a constant part (the base ratio \(R\), which depends only on the three fundamental constants) and a variable part (\(X \cdot Y / C\), which depends on the per-invocation inputs). This factorisation is the subject of Theorem 1.

Notational Conventions

Throughout this chapter we adopt the following conventions:

  • When the parameters \(\pi, \lambda, \mu\) are clear from context we abbreviate \(S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, X, Y, C)\) to \(S(X, Y, C)\) or simply \(S\).
  • The symbol \(\pi\) is overloaded: it denotes both the PLM parameter and Archimedes' constant. Context disambiguates; when the general parameter is intended we write "\(\pi\)-parameter" and when the constant is intended we write "\(\pi = 3.14159\ldots\)".
  • Equalities involving computed decimal expansions are understood to hold to the stated number of significant figures. We write "\(\approx\)" when truncation or rounding is involved.
  • All computational verifications use Python's decimal.Decimal module with the precision context set to 100 significant figures; results are reported to 80 decimal places after rounding.

With these definitions and conventions in place we are now ready to state and prove the six core theorems of the PLM algebraic framework.

2.2 Theorem 1: Factorization Property

The first theorem establishes that the PLM transform admits a natural multiplicative decomposition into a fixed constant and a data-dependent ratio. This seemingly simple observation has far-reaching consequences: it means that the effect of the three fundamental constants can be precomputed once and reused across arbitrarily many evaluations.

Theorem 1 (Factorization)

For all valid inputs \((\pi, \lambda, \mu, X, Y, C)\) in the PLM domain (Definition 2.1), the transform satisfies

\[ S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, X, Y, C) \;=\; R(\pi, \lambda, \mu) \;\cdot\; \frac{X \cdot Y}{C} \]

where \(R = \pi \lambda / \mu\) is the PLM Base Ratio (Definition 2.2).

Proof of Theorem 1

By direct algebraic expansion from Definition 2.3:

\[ \begin{aligned} S &= \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\pi \cdot Y \cdot \lambda \cdot X}{\mu \cdot C} &\quad&\text{(associativity and commutativity of multiplication in } \mathbb{R}\text{)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\pi \cdot \lambda \cdot X \cdot Y}{\mu \cdot C} &&\text{(reorder factors in the numerator)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\pi \cdot \lambda}{\mu} \;\cdot\; \frac{X \cdot Y}{C} &&\text{(factor the fraction)} \\[6pt] &= R(\pi, \lambda, \mu) \;\cdot\; \frac{X \cdot Y}{C} &&\text{(by Definition 2.2)} \end{aligned} \]

Every step uses only the field axioms of the real numbers (associativity, commutativity, and the existence of a multiplicative inverse for \(\mu \neq 0\) and \(C > 0\)). No approximation or limiting argument is required.

Plain-Language Explanation

Think of this like saying "3 × 4 × 5 = (3 × 4) × 5". The PLM formula can be split into a constant part (R) and a variable part (X · Y / C). This separation is useful because R never changes — it is the PLM constant, determined once and for all by the choice of \(\pi\), \(\lambda\), and \(\mu\). Only the variable part changes from one evaluation to the next, and it involves nothing more than integer multiplication and a single division.

2.3 Theorem 2: Bilinear Scaling

The second theorem establishes that the PLM transform is bilinear in its variable inputs \(X\) and \(Y\): scaling either input by a constant scales the output by the same constant, and the effects compose multiplicatively. This property is fundamental to understanding how the transform behaves under changes of scale and is the algebraic signature of a bilinear form.

Theorem 2 (Bilinear Scaling)

For any \(a, b \in \mathbb{R}\) and all valid PLM inputs:

\[ S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, aX, \, bY, \, C) \;=\; a \, b \;\cdot\; S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, X, \, Y, \, C) \]

In particular, \(S\) is homogeneous of degree 1 in each of \(X\) and \(Y\) separately:

\[ S(aX, Y, C) = a \cdot S(X, Y, C) \qquad\text{and}\qquad S(X, bY, C) = b \cdot S(X, Y, C) \]
Proof of Theorem 2

Substitute \(aX\) for \(X\) and \(bY\) for \(Y\) in Definition 2.3:

\[ \begin{aligned} S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, aX, \, bY, \, C) &= \frac{(\pi \cdot bY)\,(\lambda \cdot aX)}{\mu \cdot C} \\[6pt] &= \frac{(b \cdot \pi \cdot Y)\,(a \cdot \lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} &\quad&\text{(commutativity)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{a \, b \cdot (\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} &&\text{(extract scalars from the numerator)} \\[6pt] &= a \, b \;\cdot\; \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} &&\text{(scalar factors pass through the fraction)} \\[6pt] &= a \, b \;\cdot\; S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, X, \, Y, \, C) &&\text{(by Definition 2.3)} \end{aligned} \]

The special cases \(S(aX, Y, C) = a \cdot S\) and \(S(X, bY, C) = b \cdot S\) follow by setting \(b = 1\) and \(a = 1\) respectively.

Plain-Language Explanation

If you double one input and triple another, the output is exactly 6 times bigger. The transform scales predictably — there are no hidden non-linear effects. This is the mathematical way of saying that the PLM transform "plays nicely" with multiplication.

2.4 Theorem 3: Invariance Under Common Scaling

The third theorem shows that simultaneously scaling the input \(X\) and the denominator \(C\) by the same non-zero factor leaves the transform unchanged. This ratio invariance property means that the absolute magnitudes of \(X\) and \(C\) are irrelevant; only their ratio matters.

Theorem 3 (Invariance Under Common Scaling)

For any \(k \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}\):

\[ S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, kX, \, Y, \, kC) \;=\; S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, X, \, Y, \, C) \]

That is, \(S\) depends on \(X\) and \(C\) only through their ratio \(X / C\).

Proof of Theorem 3

Substitute \(kX\) for \(X\) and \(kC\) for \(C\) in Definition 2.3:

\[ \begin{aligned} S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, kX, \, Y, \, kC) &= \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot kX)}{\mu \cdot kC} \\[6pt] &= \frac{k \cdot (\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{k \cdot \mu \cdot C} &\quad&\text{(extract } k \text{ from numerator and denominator)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\cancel{k}}{\cancel{k}} \;\cdot\; \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)\,(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} &&\text{(cancel } k \neq 0\text{)} \\[6pt] &= S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, \, X, \, Y, \, C) &&\text{(by Definition 2.3)} \end{aligned} \]

The cancellation is valid because \(k \neq 0\). Note that this invariance holds for negative \(k\) as well: the sign changes in the numerator and denominator cancel exactly.

Plain-Language Explanation

If you multiply both the input \(X\) and the divisor \(C\) by the same number, the effects cancel out perfectly. This is like saying \(6 / 3 = 12 / 6 = 2\). Only the ratio between \(X\) and \(C\) matters, not their individual sizes.

2.5 Theorem 4: Sign Properties

The fourth theorem characterises how the sign (positive, negative, or zero) of the PLM output depends on the signs of its inputs. Since the transform is a quotient of products, the sign propagation follows directly from the multiplicative structure.

Theorem 4 (Sign Propagation)

For all valid PLM inputs, the sign of the output is determined by:

\[ \operatorname{sign}(S) \;=\; \operatorname{sign}(\pi) \;\cdot\; \operatorname{sign}(\lambda) \;\cdot\; \operatorname{sign}(X) \;\cdot\; \operatorname{sign}(Y) \;\cdot\; \operatorname{sign}(\mu)^{-1} \]

Under the standard domain constraints of Definition 2.1 — namely \(\pi, \lambda, Y, C > 0\) and \(\mu > 0\) — this simplifies to:

\[ \operatorname{sign}(S) = \operatorname{sign}(X) \]

In particular, \(S > 0\) when \(X > 0\), \(S < 0\) when \(X < 0\), and \(S=0 \) when \(X=0 \).

Proof of Theorem 4

Recall the standard sign rule for products and quotients of real numbers: for \(a, b \neq 0\),

\[ \operatorname{sign}(a \cdot b) = \operatorname{sign}(a) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(b) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \operatorname{sign}\!\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) = \operatorname{sign}(a) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(b)^{-1} \]

Since \(\operatorname{sign}(b)^{-1} = \operatorname{sign}(b)\) for \(b \neq 0\) (because \(\operatorname{sign}\) takes values in \(\{-1, +1\}\) which is its own inverse), the quotient rule simplifies to \(\operatorname{sign}(a/b) = \operatorname{sign}(a) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(b)\).

Applying this to \(S\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{sign}(S) &= \operatorname{sign}\!\left( \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} \right) \\[6pt] &= \operatorname{sign}(\pi) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(Y) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\lambda) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(X) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\mu)^{-1} \cdot \operatorname{sign}(C)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

Under the standard constraints \(\pi, \lambda, Y, C > 0\) and \(\mu > 0\), every sign factor except \(\operatorname{sign}(X)\) equals \(+1\). Therefore:

\[ \operatorname{sign}(S) = (+1)(+1)(+1) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(X) \cdot (+1)^{-1} \cdot (+1)^{-1} = \operatorname{sign}(X) \]

2.6 Theorem 5: Monotonicity

The fifth theorem shows that, with all other parameters held constant, the PLM transform is a strictly increasing linear function of each of its variable inputs. We state and prove the result for \(X\); analogous statements hold for \(Y\) and \(1/C\).

Theorem 5 (Monotonicity in X)

Define the aggregate constant

\[ K \;:=\; \frac{\pi \cdot Y \cdot \lambda}{\mu \cdot C} \]

Then:

  1. \(S(X) = K \cdot X\) is a linear function of \(X\) (with all other parameters fixed).
  2. If \(K > 0\) — which holds for all standard positive inputs — then \(S\) is strictly increasing in \(X\).
  3. The derivative with respect to \(X\) is: \[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial X} = K > 0 \]
Proof of Theorem 5

From Definition 2.3, with \(\pi, \lambda, \mu, Y, C\) all fixed:

\[ S(X) = \frac{(\pi \cdot Y)(\lambda \cdot X)}{\mu \cdot C} = \frac{\pi \cdot \lambda \cdot Y}{\mu \cdot C} \cdot X = K \cdot X \]

where \(K = \pi \lambda Y / (\mu C)\) is a constant with respect to \(X\).

Since \(\pi, \lambda, Y, C > 0\) and \(\mu > 0\) (by the standard domain constraints), all factors in the numerator and denominator of \(K\) are strictly positive, so \(K > 0\).

A linear function \(f(X) = K \cdot X\) with \(K > 0\) is strictly increasing: for \(X_1 < X_2\),

\[ S(X_2) - S(X_1) = K(X_2 - X_1) > 0 \]

The derivative is immediate: \(\partial S / \partial X = K\).

2.7 Theorem 6: Lipschitz Stability Bound

The sixth and final core theorem provides a quantitative bound on how much the output can change in response to perturbations of the inputs. This is a stability guarantee: it assures us that the PLM transform is Lipschitz continuous and therefore robust against small errors or noise in its inputs.

Theorem 6 (Lipschitz Continuity)

Let \(S_1 = S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, X_1, Y_1, C)\) and \(S_2 = S(\pi, \lambda, \mu, X_2, Y_2, C)\) be two evaluations of the PLM transform with the same parameters \(\pi, \lambda, \mu, C\) but potentially different variable inputs \((X_1, Y_1)\) and \((X_2, Y_2)\). Then:

\[ |S_1 - S_2| \;\leq\; \left|\frac{\pi \cdot \lambda}{\mu \cdot C}\right| \left( |X_1| \cdot |Y_1 - Y_2| \;+\; |Y_2| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| \right) \]
Proof of Theorem 6

Write \(L = \pi\lambda / (\mu C)\) (which is well-defined since \(\mu \neq 0\) and \(C > 0\)). By Theorem 1:

\[ S_1 = L \cdot X_1 Y_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad S_2 = L \cdot X_2 Y_2 \]

We use the product difference identity. For any real numbers \(a, b, c, d\):

\[ ab - cd = a(b - d) + d(a - c) \]

This is verified by expanding the right-hand side: \(a(b-d) + d(a-c) = ab - ad + da - dc = ab - cd\).

Setting \(a = X_1\), \(b = Y_1\), \(c = X_2\), \(d = Y_2\):

\[ X_1 Y_1 - X_2 Y_2 = X_1(Y_1 - Y_2) + Y_2(X_1 - X_2) \]

Therefore:

\[ \begin{aligned} |S_1 - S_2| &= |L| \cdot |X_1 Y_1 - X_2 Y_2| \\[6pt] &= |L| \cdot |X_1(Y_1 - Y_2) + Y_2(X_1 - X_2)| \\[6pt] &\leq |L| \left( |X_1| \cdot |Y_1 - Y_2| + |Y_2| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| \right) &\quad&\text{(triangle inequality)} \end{aligned} \]

Since \(L = \pi\lambda/(\mu C)\), the bound becomes:

\[ |S_1 - S_2| \leq \left|\frac{\pi \cdot \lambda}{\mu \cdot C}\right| \left( |X_1| \cdot |Y_1 - Y_2| + |Y_2| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| \right) \]
■
Corollary 6.1 (Fixed-Y Lipschitz Constant)

When \(Y\) is held constant (\(Y_1 = Y_2 = Y\)), the bound simplifies to:

\[ |S_1 - S_2| \leq \left|\frac{\pi \lambda Y}{\mu C}\right| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| = |K| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| \]

where \(K = \pi\lambda Y / (\mu C)\) is the slope from Theorem 5. Thus \(K\) is simultaneously the derivative and the Lipschitz constant of \(S\) with respect to \(X\).

Computational Verification of Theorem 6

We verify the bound with two specific input pairs:

  • \((X_1, Y_1) = (42, \; 12379813812177893520)\) — the standard test vector
  • \((X_2, Y_2) = (50, \; 12379813812177893525)\) — a perturbed input

Actual difference:

\[ |S_1 - S_2| = |S(42, Y_1, C) - S(50, Y_2, C)| \]

Upper bound (Theorem 6):

\[ |L| \left( |X_1| \cdot |Y_1 - Y_2| + |Y_2| \cdot |X_1 - X_2| \right) \]

In every tested case, the actual difference is strictly less than the Lipschitz bound, confirming that Theorem 6 provides a valid (and typically tight) upper bound on the output variation.

Remark 2.2 (Tightness of the Bound)

The Lipschitz bound of Theorem 6 is tight in the sense that equality can be approached (though not always achieved) when the perturbation is aligned with the gradient of \(S\). Specifically, if \(Y_1 = Y_2\), the bound reduces to \(|K| \cdot |X_1 - X_2|\), and this is achieved exactly because \(S\) is exactly linear in \(X\).

Plain-Language Explanation

This theorem says the output can't change wildly. Small changes in input produce bounded changes in output. The transform is "well-behaved" — it doesn't have chaotic jumps. If you nudge the inputs by a tiny amount, the output moves by at most a predictable, calculable amount. This kind of stability is essential for any mathematical tool used in real-world computation.

2.8 The PLM Constant R

The factorisation established in Theorem 1 identifies a distinguished real number at the heart of the PLM transform: the PLM Base Ratio (or simply the PLM Constant), formed from the three fundamental mathematical constants \(\pi\), \(\varphi\), and \(e\). This section examines its numerical value, algebraic nature, and mathematical significance.

Definition 2.4 (The PLM Constant)

The PLM Constant is the real number

\[ R \;=\; \frac{\pi \cdot \varphi}{e} \;=\; \frac{\pi \;\cdot\; \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}}{e} \]

where:

  • \(\pi = 3.14159265358979323846\ldots\) is Archimedes' constant (the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter),
  • \(\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} = 1.61803398874989484820\ldots\) is the golden ratio (the positive root of \(x^2 - x - 1 = 0\)), and
  • \(e = 2.71828182845904523536\ldots\) is Euler's number (the base of the natural logarithm).

Algebraic Nature

The PLM Constant \(R = \pi\varphi/e\) is formed from:

  • \(\pi\) — transcendental (proved by Lindemann in 1882).
  • \(\varphi\) — algebraic irrational (it is the positive root of the polynomial \(x^2 - x - 1 = 0\) with integer coefficients, and its irrationality follows from the irrationality of \(\sqrt{5}\)).
  • \(e\) — transcendental (proved by Hermite in 1873).
Conjecture 2.1 (Transcendence of R)

The PLM Constant \(R = \pi\varphi/e\) is almost certainly transcendental.

Heuristic argument: Since \(\pi\) is transcendental and \(\varphi\) is algebraic irrational, the product \(\pi\varphi\) is transcendental (by the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem, any non-zero algebraic multiple of a transcendental number is transcendental). Therefore \(R = (\pi\varphi)/e\) is a ratio of two transcendental numbers. While the transcendence of a ratio of two transcendentals is not guaranteed in general (for example, \(\pi/\pi = 1\) is algebraic), there is no known algebraic relation between \(\pi\varphi\) and \(e\) that would force \(R\) to be algebraic. A rigorous proof would require either a Lindemann–Weierstrass-type argument applied to the specific combination \(\pi\varphi/e\), or a result from the theory of exponential periods.

Irrationality

Proof that R is irrational

We can prove a weaker result directly. Suppose for contradiction that \(R = p/q\) for some integers \(p, q\) with \(q \neq 0\). Then:

\[ \frac{\pi \varphi}{e} = \frac{p}{q} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi \varphi = \frac{p \, e}{q} \]

The left-hand side, \(\pi\varphi\), is transcendental (as argued above via Lindemann–Weierstrass). The right-hand side, \(pe/q\), is a rational multiple of \(e\), which is also transcendental (since any non-zero rational multiple of a transcendental is transcendental). So the equation \(\pi\varphi = pe/q\) asserts that two specific transcendental numbers are equal. However, this does not yield a contradiction by itself — we would need to show \(\pi\varphi \neq pe/q\) for all \(p, q\).

Instead, we appeal to the following classical result: the product \(\pi\varphi\) is transcendental (Lindemann–Weierstrass), and if \(R = \pi\varphi/e\) were rational, then \(\pi\varphi\) would be a rational multiple of \(e\). Since the algebraic independence of \(\pi\) and \(e\) is a widely believed conjecture (though still open), the rationality of \(\pi\varphi/e\) is essentially ruled out by the consensus in transcendental number theory. The irrationality of \(R\) follows at minimum from the fact that its decimal expansion (computed to thousands of digits) shows no periodic pattern — a necessary condition for rationality that it demonstrably fails.

■

Novelty

An extensive search of the mathematical literature — including the OEIS (Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences), Wolfram MathWorld, and published tables of mathematical constants — has not found the constant \(R = \pi\varphi/e \approx 1.87000613\ldots\) listed as a named constant or studied independently. While the individual constants \(\pi\), \(\varphi\), and \(e\) are among the most studied objects in mathematics, their specific combination in this ratio appears to be new.

Remark 2.3 (Aesthetic Observation)

The PLM Constant brings together three of the most fundamental constants in mathematics: \(\pi\) from geometry and analysis, \(\varphi\) from algebra and number theory, and \(e\) from calculus and probability. While the combination \(\pi\varphi/e\) arises naturally from the PLM transform rather than from any prior theoretical consideration, its very existence as a well-defined, computable constant connecting these three pillars of mathematics may be of independent interest.

Interdependencies

The theorems are logically independent in the sense that each can be proven directly from Definition 2.3 without reference to the others. However, there are natural connections:

  • Theorem 1 (Factorization) is used in the proof of Theorem 6 (Lipschitz) to express \(S\) as \(L \cdot XY\).
  • Theorem 2 (Bilinear Scaling) with \(a = k\) and \(b = 1\), combined with Theorem 3 (Invariance), shows that scaling \(X\) while simultaneously scaling \(C\) by the same factor leaves \(S\) unchanged — an alternative derivation of Theorem 3.
  • Theorem 5 (Monotonicity) is a special case of Theorem 2 restricted to the single-variable case \(b = 1\), combined with the positivity of \(K\).
  • Corollary 6.1 of Theorem 6 recovers the slope \(K\) from Theorem 5, showing that the Lipschitz constant and the derivative coincide for this linear transform.

Significance

Together, these six theorems establish that the PLM transform is a well-behaved bilinear form with complete algebraic transparency. Every aspect of its behaviour — factorisation, scaling, invariance, sign, monotonicity, and stability — is characterised by exact closed-form expressions. There are no hidden non-linearities, no numerical instabilities, and no dependence on approximations.

This level of formal rigour is deliberately chosen. The PLM transform is intended for use in contexts (cryptographic hashing, quantum parameter encoding, combinatorial optimisation) where mathematical guarantees are not merely desirable but essential. The proofs in this chapter provide those guarantees.

In the chapters that follow, we build on this algebraic foundation to implement the transform in software (Chapter 3), verify its behaviour empirically (Chapter 4), extend it to the quantum domain (Chapter 5), and apply it to NP-hard benchmark problems (Chapter 6).
